You can **miss a train** and **miss a friend**. Dutch uses one word for both, too: *missen*. If you put only “missen — to miss” on a card, you may remember the translation without knowing which situation to use it in.

Let’s look at *missen* in **Dilum Nederlands**, our learner’s dictionary. The entry has five meanings. We’ll take two of them and make a few practice questions.

## Start with a meaning

The dictionary explains the first meaning like this:

> **Iets niet bijwonen of niet meemaken; ook: een vervoermiddel niet halen.**

In other words, you don’t attend or experience something, or you’re too late to catch a bus, train or other transport. Here are two examples from the entry:

- **Door de vertraging miste ze het begin van de film.** Because of the delay, she missed the beginning of the film.
- **Ik wil de laatste trein niet missen.** I don’t want to miss the last train.

Now compare these notes:

> **Before:** missen — to miss.
>
> **After:** missen — to miss a train or other transport. Example: *Ik wil de laatste trein niet missen.*

The second note gives you a specific meaning and a sentence that shows how to use it.

## Turn the example into a question

To check your understanding, you could ask:

> **Question:** Ik wil de laatste trein niet missen. What does the speaker want to avoid?
>
> **Answer:** Missing the last train.

To practise recalling the Dutch word, try a gap:

> **Question:** Fill in the verb meaning “to miss the train”: Ik wil de laatste trein niet ___.
>
> **Answer:** missen.

The clue makes the intended verb clear. Without it, other words could also fit the sentence.

## The same word in another situation

*Missen* can also mean feeling sad because someone or something is absent. Here’s an example from Dilum Nederlands:

> **Na haar verhuizing mist ze haar oude vrienden.**
>
> After moving, she misses her old friends.

Here, *mist* is a form of *missen*. You can check it and other forms in the [dictionary from the Dutch Language Institute](https://anw.ivdnt.org/article/missen).

If the meanings get mixed up, make a separate question for each one. One card can ask about the train; another can ask whom the person misses. The context helps you work out what’s happening.

## What to put on your card

Take a word you want to practise and write down:

1. **A meaning** you want to remember.
2. **An example** that makes the meaning clear.
3. **A question and answer** so you can check yourself.

Cover the answer and read the question. If more than one answer fits, add context or include the other acceptable answers on the back.
