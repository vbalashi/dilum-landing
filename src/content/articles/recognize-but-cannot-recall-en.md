In an experiment by Akifumi Yanagisawa, 18 students learned made-up English words. Each word came with a picture that showed what it meant. When the students later saw the pictures on a test, they were more likely to name the words they’d already practised recalling from the pictures. Sounds obvious? It worked. The trick is to practise before you need the word. [Yanagisawa’s study, 2016](https://doi.org/10.20806/katejournal.30.0_139).

The other day I took the KNM exam, the Dutch society part of my A2 integration exams. One question was about a father and his child. I’d seen the word *opvoeden* plenty of times, but I drew a blank. Was the father raising his child? Or did the question have something to do with paying for the child’s education?

I never did remember. As soon as I got out, I found out that *opvoeden* means ‘to raise’ or ‘to bring up’. Well, there goes one question.

![Mobile card for opvoeden in Dilum Nederlands, with the English translation](/images/a027-opvoeden-en-mobile.jpg)

I was glad I remembered plenty of other words, though. I’d made my own KNM vocabulary list in 2000nl and split it into eight topics. I still started later than I should have, but I got enough practice in. We only recently added collections, and I was surprised by how handy they were.

![Eight KNM vocabulary collections in the 2000nl mobile library](/images/a027-knm-mobile.jpg)

I can’t take credit for the idea. You put together a list for yourself once, and someone else can pick it up and start practising right away. The KNM collection is ready to try. You can make your own collections, too.
